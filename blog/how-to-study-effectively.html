Study Skills

How to Study Effectively: 7 Evidence-Based Techniques

By EduPath Team · Nov 1, 2026 · 6 min read

Many students study for hours but still forget most of it by exam day. The problem is usually not effort, it is method. Re-reading notes and highlighting feel productive, but research shows they help very little. In 2013, a team of psychologists led by John Dunlosky reviewed hundreds of studies on ten popular study techniques. Only a few earned a "high utility" rating. This article explains seven techniques backed by that kind of evidence, and how you can use each one today.

1. Active Recall (Test Yourself)

Active recall means pulling information out of your memory instead of reading it again. Close the book and try to write down or say what you remember. In a well-known 2006 experiment, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke found that students who tested themselves remembered much more after one week than students who simply re-read the same text.

How to use it: after each topic, close your notes and answer questions such as "What are the three causes of…?" Flashcards and past papers are simple ways to do this.

2. Spaced Practice (Don't Cram)

Studying the same topic in several short sessions spread over days works better than one long session the night before. This is called the spacing effect. A large review by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues (2006) found it across hundreds of studies. Each time you return to a topic after a gap, your brain has to work a little harder, and that makes the memory stronger.

How to use it: review new material after one day, then after three days, then after a week. Write these review dates in your planner.

3. Interleaving (Mix Your Topics)

Interleaving means mixing different types of problems in one session instead of practising one type many times in a row. For example, in maths, mix area, volume and percentage questions. It feels harder, but studies on maths learning by Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor showed better test scores, because students learn to choose the right method, not just repeat it.

How to use it: when you practise, shuffle questions from two or three chapters together.

4. Elaboration (Ask "Why?" and "How?")

Elaboration means explaining ideas in more detail and connecting them to what you already know. Asking yourself "Why is this true?" or "How does this connect to last week's lesson?" builds a web of links in memory, which makes the idea easier to find later.

How to use it: for each key fact, write one sentence that explains why it happens or how it relates to another topic.

5. Explain It Simply

If you can explain a topic in simple words, you understand it. If you get stuck, you have found a gap. Researchers call this self-explanation, and it is the idea behind the popular "teach it to a friend" method.

How to use it: pretend you are teaching a younger student. Explain the topic out loud or on paper without looking at your notes, then check what you missed.

6. Use Words and Pictures Together

Combining text with visuals such as diagrams, timelines or mind maps gives your brain two ways to remember the same idea. This approach is known as dual coding. It works especially well for processes, for example how the heart pumps blood or how a law is passed.

How to use it: turn a page of notes into one simple diagram, then explain the diagram in your own words.

7. Protect Your Sleep and Focus

Sleep is when the brain strengthens new memories. Research reviews on sleep and memory, such as Diekelmann and Born (2010), show that people remember more when they sleep after learning. Staying up all night to cram works against you. Distractions matter too: switching between study and your phone makes learning slower and weaker.

How to use it: aim for regular sleep, especially before exams. Study in focused blocks of 25–45 minutes with your phone in another room, then take a short break.

A Simple Weekly Plan

  • Day 1: learn a new topic, then test yourself on it.
  • Day 2: quick recall review (10–15 minutes).
  • Day 4: mixed practice questions with older topics.
  • Day 7: explain the whole topic without notes.

Conclusion

Studying effectively is not about studying longer. It is about using methods that make your brain work: testing yourself, spacing your reviews, mixing topics, asking why, explaining simply, using visuals and getting enough sleep. Start with one or two techniques this week, and add more as they become habits.
