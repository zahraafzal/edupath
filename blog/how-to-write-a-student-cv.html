How to Write a Student CV With Little Experience

By EduPath Team · Nov 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Writing your first CV can feel difficult when you have little or no work experience. The good news is that employers and internship programmes do not expect students to have long careers. They want to see your potential: what you have learned, what you have done and how you work with others. This guide shows you how to present that clearly.

Keep It Short and Clean

A student CV should usually be one page. Use a simple layout, a readable font, clear headings and consistent dates. Many companies use applicant tracking systems (ATS) that read your CV automatically, so avoid complex tables, images and text inside graphics.

The Sections to Include

  • Contact details: name, phone, a professional email, city and a LinkedIn or portfolio link.
  • Profile: two or three lines about who you are and what role you are looking for.
  • Education: degree, institution, dates and relevant results or courses.
  • Projects: university, personal or group projects with a short result.
  • Experience: internships, part-time jobs, freelancing, tutoring or volunteering.
  • Skills: technical skills, tools and languages.
  • Achievements and activities: awards, competitions, clubs and leadership roles.

Show Skills Through Projects

If you do not have a job yet, projects are your experience. For each project, write what you made, the tools you used and the result. For example: "Built a budget-tracking website with HTML, CSS and JavaScript used by 40 classmates." Real details are more convincing than a list of adjectives.

Write Strong Bullet Points

Start each bullet point with an action verb and add a number where you can:

  • Weak: "Responsible for social media of the society."
  • Strong: "Managed the society's Instagram page and grew followers from 300 to 1,200 in six months."

Useful action verbs include organised, built, led, analysed, designed, taught and improved.

Tailor Your CV for Each Application

Read the job or internship description carefully. Move the most relevant projects and skills to the top, and use some of the same keywords the employer uses, as long as they are true for you. A tailored CV is far more effective than sending the same document everywhere.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Spelling and grammar errors. Read it aloud and ask someone to check it.
  • Unprofessional email addresses.
  • Long paragraphs instead of short bullet points.
  • Listing skills you cannot actually demonstrate in an interview.
  • Adding personal details that are not needed, such as ID numbers.
  • Saving the file with a vague name. Use "FirstName-LastName-CV.pdf".

Add a Short Cover Letter

When possible, send a short cover letter or email with your CV. In three short paragraphs, say which role you are applying for, why you are interested, and which one or two experiences make you a good fit.

Conclusion

A strong student CV is short, honest and specific. Focus on projects, results and skills you can prove, and adjust it for every application. Still deciding which direction to take? Read our guide Choosing a Career After Graduation.
